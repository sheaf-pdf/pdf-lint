# Changelog

## 0.1.0-alpha.1 (unreleased)

The first release, and an alpha: until a release says it's stable, any alpha may change the API, each saying here what changed and how code follows. A crate that depends on an alpha names it exactly, `=0.1.0-alpha.1`: cargo's default, `0.1.0-alpha.1`, takes any later alpha of 0.1.0 too.

- **The objects.** `Object`, a `Value` (null, boolean, integer, real, name, string, array, dictionary, stream, reference) and where it was written, a `Span` of the file's bytes or of a stream's decoded data, an object stream's for an object in one, when the reader knows. `Value::Stream` holds a `Box<Stream>`, so that a value of any other kind isn't a stream's size. A `Dict` keeps its entries as written, in order, a key written twice and each key's span among them: lookups take a key's last entry, `duplicates` gives the others. A `Str` is its bytes; or text a reader decoded without saying from which encoding; or ciphertext it didn't decrypt. A string keeps its `Form`, literal or hex, beside its `Str` (`Value::String(Str, Option<Form>)`): none for one built without choosing (`Object::string`, `Object::bytes`; `Object::hex` chooses hex) or read by a reader that doesn't say. `dict!` builds a dictionary from keys and values. `Span` is `#[non_exhaustive]`, made by `Span::new`, so a field can come without a breaking release.
- **Ids.** `ObjectId` is `struct ObjectId { number: u32, generation: u16 }` (ISO 32000-2, 7.3.10): `Copy`, ordered and hashed by number and then generation, eight bytes; built by `ObjectId::new(n, g)`, a `const fn`, or a literal; written by `Display` as a file writes it, `12 0`, so `format!("{id} R")` is a reference and `format!("{id} obj")` a header, and `Debug` the derive's. A pair `(u32, u16)` converts both ways through `From`, for an object model that spells its ids so.
- **Editing.** An `Object`'s value is private: `value` reads it and `into_value` takes it; `value_mut`, `as_dict_mut`, `as_array_mut` and `as_stream_mut` change it, each an edit, which drops its span, and as a value inside another is reached through each value that holds it, theirs go too, while a value no edit reached keeps its own. `as_written_mut` changes it and keeps the span, for a reader that turns a value into what the same bytes stand for, as decrypting a string does. `as_bool`, `as_bytes` (a string's bytes), `as_array` and `as_stream` read by kind, beside `as_name`, `as_integer`, `as_number`, `as_dict` (a stream's dictionary too) and `as_reference`. An `Object` comes from an `ObjectId` (a reference), any integer type of 32 bits or fewer and `i64`, an `f32` (the f64 nearest its shortest decimal, so `0.1f32` is 0.1) and a `String` (a name), beside the rest. A `Dict` is changed by `set`, `push`, `get_mut` and `iter_mut` (a key's last entry) and `remove`, which keeps the other entries in order and gives the value of the last entry it removed, and `remove_duplicates`, which removes the entries a later one overrides; `has_type` and `type_name` read its `/Type`; it's collected from keys and values, each pushed, so a key given twice is kept twice; and a `for` over a `&Dict` is `iter`, whose iterator is `object::Iter`. Its lookups allocate nothing for a dictionary of up to 32 entries; past 32, a walk finds the keys written again in a set made with room for every key. A real's spelling is the bytes its span names.
- **Each walk is a type of its own.** `Dict::iter`, `iter_mut`, `last_entries` and `duplicates` give `object::Iter`, `IterMut`, `LastEntries` and `Duplicates`, and `Object::references` an `object::References`, so a caller can hold one in a struct or name it in a signature; `Dict::entries_mut` gives the slice's own `std::slice::IterMut<Entry>`. Each is fused, the dictionary's walks from either end too, and `Debug`; all but `IterMut` are `Clone`. Each method of theirs is a hint to inline, as `core`'s own adapters' are, so that a consumer's loop over one is one loop.
- **A stream's data.** `Stream::data` is a `Data`, `#[non_exhaustive]`: `At`, where a reader read it; `Bytes`, in memory, as `Stream::new` and `set_bytes` give it, setting `/Length`; or `Unplaced`. `Data::at` and `Data::bytes` read it. An edit of the stream's dictionary leaves its data as it was. `Stream` is `#[non_exhaustive]` too, made outside the crate by `Stream::new`, `Object::stream` or from `Stream::default()`.
- **A stream's data, decoded.** `Streams`, at the root and in `streams`, is what gives a stream's data, decrypted and decoded through its filters, as a viewer takes it: `decoded(id, limit) -> Result<Cow<[u8]>, Unreadable>`, at most `limit` bytes, what decoded of a stream that decodes in part unless the limit stopped it, borrowed where the reader holds the data decoded and owned where it's decoded for the call; or why there's none, in a resolver's words. A reference to one is one (`&S`, `&dyn Streams`). `WithStreams`, at the root and in `streams`, is objects with their streams' data by a function, as pdf-structure's `WithReading` is a document with its reading: a resolver and a function from a stream's number and a limit to its data, `Result<Vec<u8>, Unreadable>`, joined (`WithStreams::new(objects, |id, limit| ..)`), `Resolve` by the first and `Streams` by the second, `Copy` where they are; what the function gives is given as it is, for the caller to hold to the limit. Two of `Why`'s answers only a stream's data gives, never a resolver: `Why::Filter`, a stream whose filters the reader doesn't decode, through a filter it doesn't take, as an image's data may be (ISO 32000-2, 7.4.1), its data there as written, or with a `/Filter` or `/DecodeParms` it can't take as written; and `Why::Detached`, a stream's data the reader doesn't hold and has nothing to read from, as a document read whole alone hasn't the file's bytes (pdf-structure's `Document`), which asked again gives the same: read it beside what read the file. `Why::Source` is apart from it: the source of the file's bytes didn't give them, which asked again may read. `Why` is `#[non_exhaustive]`, so a match on it has a wildcard.
- **Faults, told with where they are.** `fault`, a module, holds `Sink<F>` and `Place` for every reader of a file's objects that tells what it reads past: `Sink::fault(at, fault)` takes the reader's own fault type `F` and its `Place`, the numbered object it's in and where it was written (`#[non_exhaustive]`, made by `Place::new`, a `const fn`); `Sink::wants`, true unless a sink says otherwise, says whether faults are wanted at all. `()` drops them and wants none, so a reader asked for none looks for none; a sink borrowed, `&mut S` or `&mut dyn Sink<F>`, is one; a `Vec<(Place, F)>` keeps each with its place, in the order told. Generic over the fault, so one sink type takes several readers' faults by implementing `Sink<A>` and `Sink<B>`. pdf-semantics', pdf-color's and pdf-fonts' faults are told to it. The parser keeps its own `parse::Sink`, its faults borrowing the data parsed, each at a `Span` alone. `fault` is reached as a module, not at the root, so that `fault::Sink` and `parse::Sink` each say which they are.
- **References resolved.** `Resolve<'o>`, at the root, is what gives the indirect object a reference names, `resolve(id) -> Option<&'o Object>`, none being null. The objects' lifetime is the trait's, not `&self`'s, so that a closure giving borrows of its own is one: every `Fn(ObjectId) -> Option<&Object>` is, by a blanket impl, a `dyn Fn` and a reference or a box of one among them; a type that holds the objects implements it for a reference to itself. A `&dyn Resolve` is one too, for what takes a resolver by value. `try_resolve(id) -> Result<Option<&'o Object>, Unreadable>`, by default what `resolve` gives, says why where the resolver can, as a reader's and a document's do: none when the file hasn't the object, or an `Unreadable` (`#[non_exhaustive]`, made by `Unreadable::new`, derived with thiserror): the object, its `Why` (`#[non_exhaustive]`: `Damaged`, `Limit`, `Locked` or `Source`, and for a stream's data `Filter` and `Detached`, above) and the reader's words.
- **References walked.** `Object::references` gives, as an `object::References`, the ids of the objects a value's references name, nested ones too, in the order written: itself when it's a reference, an array's elements, a dictionary's every entry, a key written twice included, and a stream's dictionary, never its data. It walks with a stack of its own, not by recursion, so a value nested however deeply, as one built in memory may be, walks without overflowing the thread's.
- **References renumbered, and places dropped.** `Object::renumber(to)` names each reference in a value anew, as `to`, a function from the object a reference names to the one it's to name, gives it, or null where it gives none (§7.3.10), the references `references` gives, in its order, `to` asked once each; each it changes is an edit, dropping its place and every holder's, while what it changed nothing in keeps its own, a key and a stream's data theirs; it says whether it changed any. `Object::unplace` drops every place in a value, its own, each value's and key's, and a stream's data's where it was written (`Data::At` made `Data::Unplaced`; `Data::Bytes` kept), for an object of one file put among another's, whose places would be read as that file's; a stream's data is given first as its bytes. Both walk with a stack of their own. The writer names references through a renumbering too, without changing the value: `serialize::Renumber`, implemented by `()`, each as it is, and by any function from an `ObjectId` to an `Option<ObjectId>`, `None` written as `null`; `write_renumbered`, `indirect_renumbered` and `indirect_stream_renumbered` write through one, an indirect object at the number given, which its encryptor is given too, beside its dictionary as held, its references not renumbered, and a stream's `/Length` its data's whatever reference it holds. A renumbering is a function: asked of an id many times, on several threads at once, it gives the same answer, so a numbering made as it's asked is made first and given as a map. The writers without one write as through `()`, by the walk compiled for it, so they pay for no renumbering.
- **Text strings.** `text::decode` decodes a text string by its byte order mark, UTF-16BE, UTF-8 or none for PDFDocEncoding (Annex D, `text::pdf_doc_char`), and says each `Quirk`, where it departs from the spec: UTF-16LE's mark, read as PDFDocEncoding; an odd last byte, left out; a lone surrogate or bad UTF-8, read as U+FFFD; a byte PDFDocEncoding leaves undefined. `Quirk` is `#[non_exhaustive]`, so a quirk can come without a breaking release. `decode` doesn't take out language escapes (ISO 32000-2, 7.9.2.2.2). `text::decode_with` reads by `Rules`: ISO 32000-2's, which is `decode`; or PDFium's, as of chromium/8125, where `FF FE` is UTF-16LE's mark (`Encoding::Utf16Le`; `Encoding` is `#[non_exhaustive]`, so that another could come), language escapes come out of UTF-16 and UTF-8, one with no close taking out the rest (`Quirk::UnclosedEscape`), bytes that aren't UTF-8 are left out, a sequence longer than it need be read as what it spells, and PDFDocEncoding's undefined bytes are U+0000; a surrogate alone, which a `String` can't hold, is U+FFFD by both. Each departure is said as `decode` says it. `text::Text`, a decoder's account of a text string, is `#[non_exhaustive]` too, as it may say more, such as the languages a PDFium reading takes out with their escapes: read by its fields, it can't be built or matched without `..` outside the crate.
- **Text strings written.** `text::encode` gives a text's bytes as a text string that reads back as that text, under `decode` whatever the text: as they are where PDFDocEncoding reads them back so, ASCII but for 0x18 to 0x1F and 0x7F, and otherwise UTF-16BE after its mark. `Object::text` is that string, literal as it is and hex as UTF-16BE, and what a literal must escape reads back. `text::encode_as` writes PDFDocEncoding through `pdf_doc_char`'s table, UTF-16BE, or UTF-8 after its mark, PDF 2.0's, as the caller asks, and refuses what wouldn't read back (`text::Unencodable`, `#[non_exhaustive]`, derived with thiserror): a character PDFDocEncoding hasn't; a text whose bytes there would start as a mark, `þÿ`, `ï»¿` or `ÿþ`; U+001B in the other two, where it starts a language escape; and UTF-16LE, which PDF hasn't. `encode` writes that U+001B all the same, as UTF-16BE, where a reader that takes escapes out takes it and what follows.
- **Versions.** `Version`, read from a header's or a catalog's text, displayed as `1.7`, and ordered.
- **The parser.** `parse::Parser`, on pdf-tokenizer, reads a value, or an indirect object, from a file's bytes or an object stream's decoded data (`in_stream`), from any offset, kept as given past the data's end; each value placed, each string with the form it was written in, strings read as ciphertext when asked (`ciphertext`). What it reads past is a `Fault`, borrowing the data, told to a `Sink` with where it is, which may keep it as it is; `()` is the sink that drops them, and a sink borrowed, `&mut dyn Sink` too, is one. What stops a value is an `Error`, with where, and leaves the parser where it was. An indirect object is read in steps, `header`, `body` and, for a stream, `stream`: `body` gives a stream's dictionary and both extents its data may have, `declared` by a `/Length` the caller resolves and `scanned` up to `endstream`, and the caller passes `stream` the one it chose. A window of a file (`Parser::window(base, last)`): the bytes a reader by position has read from an offset on, which the parser's spans, `offset()` and every error's place then count in the file, from `base`; where the window isn't the file's last, a call whose read reaches its end (a token reaching it, none before it where one was looked for, a stream's data or the extents it was given running to it) is `Error::Short`, as more of the file could change what it reads, and the caller reads again with more, the faults said before it said again. Read so, it reads as it would with the whole file. `Parser::at` reads its data as a whole file. `StreamHead` remembers when `declared` or `scanned` ran to a window's end, for `stream`. `Parser`, `StreamHead` and `Body` are `Send` and `Sync`. It reads under `Limits` (`Parser::limits`), `#[non_exhaustive]`, so set on a copy of `Limits::DEFAULT`: arrays and dictionaries nested at most `nesting` deep, `NESTING` (256) by default (`Error::Nested`), and at most `values` values in one call, keys among them, 2^25 by default (`Error::Values`). Only a value reaches them, and either error says where it starts; a token that can't start a value, where one should be, is the error it is under any limits. A name without `#`, which has nothing to decode and nothing to say, is its bytes after the `/`, read without walking its pieces. `Fault` and `Error` are `#[non_exhaustive]`, and both `std::error::Error`s, derived with thiserror. Shown with `{:?}`, `Parser` and `StreamHead` say where they are, the data's length and how they read it (`Parser { offset: 12, data: 4000 bytes, base: 0, .. }`), never the data, which may be a whole file, or an object stream's decrypted data; `Body`'s `Debug` is derived over them, and the values', `Object`, `Value`, `Dict`, `Stream` and `Data`, show them whole. It passes white-space and comments with pdf-tokenizer's `next_significant`, which makes no token of them, and finds a stream's `endstream` through `data_until`, memchr's search; it reads an array's element from the token the array looked at for its `]`, and looks for a reference's `R` only where an integer follows a number; and it decodes an escaped name or string once, into a buffer reserved for as many bytes as were written (pdf-tokenizer's `size_hint`), so its capacity may exceed its length by what the escapes took.
- **ISO 32000-2's rules, or PDFium's.** `Parser::rules(Rules)` (`Rules`, pdf-tokenizer's, `pdf_objects::pdf_tokenizer::Rules` through the crate, below; pdf-tokenizer 0.2 required): ISO 32000-2's lexical rules by default, or PDFium's, as of chromium/8125, a development snapshot of PDFium, any other rules read as ISO's. Each call reads by a tokenizer with the rules fixed in its type, chosen once a call, so ISO's path pays nothing for PDFium's. By PDFium's rules, tokens are cut where PDFium cuts them (0x80 and 0xFF white-space, a word of digits, signs and points a number, a hex string to its `>`), every tokenizer the parser makes following them, a stream's `endstream` after its declared data among them; and each token is read as PDFium's object parser reads it, held as this crate holds values: a number word as the integer PDFium holds (unsigned or signed in 32 bits, 0 outside them) or the decimal a real starts with, at 64 bits (`--5` 0, `1.2.3` 1.2, `4294967296` 0, `4294967295` itself); a name's `#` before any two bytes as an escape, a byte that's no hex digit as 0; a literal string's CR, and CR LF, as written; a hex string's digits paired over any other byte; a number or a name longer than 256 bytes as its first 256, a name's `/` among them. A reference's and a header's integers are their tokens' values, in this parser's grammar, `n g R` and `n g obj`, under either. Where ISO 32000-2 reads such a token otherwise, a fault, each naming its clause: `Fault::Number` (a number only by PDFium's rules, a keyword to ISO), `OutOfRange` (an integer outside PDFium's 32 bits, read as 0), `HexString` (a byte that's no hex digit), `StringCr` (a CR written in a literal string), `Long` (past 256 bytes); and `Fault::Name` where ISO's rules tell it, its doc saying how PDFium reads such a `#`. 0x80 and 0xFF read as white-space in an object are told too (`Fault::HighByte`, below). `Sink::wants`, true by default, `()` false and a borrowed sink forwarding it: a sink that wants no faults is told none, and the checks that read a token a second way to find one don't run for it.
- **A real too small for 64 bits is said.** `Fault::RealUnderflow`, "a real too small for 64 bits, read as 0", beside `RealOverflow`: by ISO 32000-2's rules a real whose digits aren't all zero but which is too small for an `f64` (`0.` and 400 zeros and a `1`) reads as 0 with its sign, and is told at its bytes where the sink wants faults (7.3.3; Annex C's range is advice). Not by PDFium's rules, which read a number from its first 256 bytes, none of which is too small (the least, `.` and 254 zeros and a `1`, is 1e-255): such a word is `Long` there. A reader that tells the parser's faults as its own warnings warns of such a real.
- **0x80 and 0xFF read as white-space are said.** `Fault::HighByte { written }`, "0x80 is white-space here and a regular character to ISO 32000-2" (7.2.3), the one byte at its place: by PDFium's rules, each 0x80 and 0xFF read as white-space in the object a call reads, from its first token to the end of its last (a value; a stream's dictionary through its `stream` keyword and end of line, by `body`; from its data's end through `endstream`, by `stream`), once, after the call's other faults, in the bytes' order. Not in a string, a comment or a stream's data, where it's a byte like any other; not around an object, before its first token or after its last, which a call may read past looking for an `R` or `endobj` (between `obj` and the value, before `endobj`, after `endstream`), being no object's; and not by a call that fails, which reads no object, a call short of a window's end among them, so the call with more tells it, once. Looked for only by PDFium's rules, fixed in the tokenizer's type, so ISO's cost nothing, and only where the sink wants faults: one search of the object's bytes for either, through memchr (as pdf-tokenizer takes it, without its default features), and its tokens read again from its first only where that finds one. The fuzz target's body holds it: each told is one such byte, never twice, and every such byte in what's read is told; and a sink that wants no faults is told none.
- **What PDFium's rules read and don't tell, said.** `Parser::header` reads by the parser's rules and tells nothing, having no sink: by PDFium's, `--5 0 obj` is object 0, and a 0x80 between its words white-space, neither said; its doc says so. `Value::Real`'s and `Parser::rules`' docs say that a real is held at 64 bits by either rules, the `f64` nearest the decimal, so `0.1` is written back as `0.1`, where PDFium holds the nearest `f32`, which the `f64` as an `f32` is but where rounding twice parts from rounding once (a real just under the halfway point past the greatest `f32`, which PDFium holds as the greatest and the `f64` rounds on to infinity); a caller that needs PDFium's own reads the word by `pdf_tokenizer::decode::pdfium::number(word).float()`.
- **The writer.** `serialize` writes a value as PDF syntax (`write`), or as an indirect object, `n g obj` to `endobj` (`indirect`), which the parser reads back as the same value, under limits that take in its count of values, and a stream as an indirect object: a real in its shortest form that reads back as the same f64, past i64's range too, never with an exponent, its magnitude clamped as Annex C has it (`real`); integers and references in decimal (`decimal`), names (`name`) and strings (`string`) byte by byte, a string in its `Form`, or literal when it has none; a dictionary's every entry, in order, a key written twice included, which ISO 32000-2 doesn't allow (`Dict::remove_duplicates` leaves each key's last); and a stream's bytes as they are, their length its `/Length`, in place of each `/Length` entry or after the others. `indirect_stream` writes a stream whose data is given apart, as it is. What it can't write as the value it refuses, writing nothing (`serialize::Error`, `#[non_exhaustive]`, derived with thiserror): a real that isn't finite, values nested deeper than the parser reads them under `Limits::DEFAULT`, a string read as text or still ciphertext, and a stream whose data isn't in memory.
- **Encrypted as it's written.** `serialize::Encrypt`, an encryptor that every string of an indirect object, a stream's dictionary's included, and a stream's data pass through as the writer writes them, given the object's number and generation: `clear`, asked once an object, leaves it whole in the clear; `clear_key`, asked once a dictionary, leaves an entry so; `string` and `stream` give what's written, as a `Cow`, a stream's data before its dictionary, so its `/Length` is the encrypted data's. `indirect_with` and `indirect_stream_with` write through one, refusing as the writer does or as the encryptor does, with nothing written (`Refused`, the two apart: `Write`, the writer's `Error`; `Encrypt`, the object and the encryptor's error; derived with thiserror, and exhaustive, as each side grows in its own error). `()` encrypts nothing (`Infallible`): `indirect` and `indirect_stream` write as through it, by a walk compiled apart with nothing to encrypt, so a file that isn't encrypted pays for no encryptor. `write`, a direct object, never encrypts. `indirect_stream`'s data is plaintext, encoded by its dictionary's filters, which `indirect_stream_with` encrypts. No cipher here: pdf-structure implements the trait over pdf-crypt.
- **pdf-tokenizer, re-exported whole.** `pdf_objects::pdf_tokenizer` is the pdf-tokenizer this crate is built with, 0.2, so the `Rules` that `Parser::rules` and `text::decode_with` take is `pdf_objects::pdf_tokenizer::Rules`, at the version this crate takes, or `pdf_tokenizer::Rules` where a crate depends on pdf-tokenizer 0.2 itself. A crate whose API names another family crate's types re-exports that crate whole, and only its own types by name, so a type is never reached by two paths that can part at a version.
- **Exhaustive on purpose, or not, and said.** `Value`, `Str`, `Form`, `parse::Body` and `serialize::Refused` are exhaustive, and so are `ObjectId`, `Entry` and `Version`, whose every field is public, each doc saying why: a match on every kind is checked by the compiler, so a new variant or field is a breaking release. What's found in files rather than designed, or may say more, is `#[non_exhaustive]`, so it can grow without one, a match on it needing a wildcard and a struct being made by the crate: `Why`, `Data`, the parser's `Fault` and `Error`, the writer's `Error`, `text::Encoding`, `Quirk`, `Unencodable` and `Text`, and `Span`, `Stream`, `Limits`, `fault::Place` and `Unreadable`. The README says so.
- **What's dropped by mistake is marked, and what can be `const` is.** `#[must_use]` on `Parser` and `StreamHead`, which do nothing until asked, beside the walks of a dictionary and of references; and on every function whose result dropped is a mistake and whose type doesn't say so itself: `Object::new`, `null`, `reference`, `string`, `text`, `bytes`, `hex`, `stream`, `at`, `value`, `into_value`, `as_bool`, `as_name`, `as_integer`, `as_number`, `as_bytes`, `as_array`, `as_dict`, `as_stream`, `as_reference` and `is_null`; `Str::text` and `as_bytes`; `Data::at` and `bytes`; `Stream::new`; `Dict::new`, `get`, `get_mut`, `entry`, `has`, `type_name`, `has_type`, `entries`, `len` and `is_empty`; `ObjectId::new`, `Span::new`, `fault::Place::new`, `Unreadable::new`, `WithStreams::new`, `Version::new` and `parse`; `Parser::offset`; `StreamHead::dict`, `keyword`, `start`, `declared` and `scanned`; `text::decode`, `decode_with`, `encode` and `pdf_doc_char`; and the traits' `Resolve::resolve`, `fault::Sink::wants`, `parse::Sink::wants`, `serialize::Encrypt::clear` and `clear_key`, and `Renumber::renumber`, which reach every implementation. None on what gives a `Result` or a marked type, or nothing, or on an edit whose result a caller may drop (`value_mut`, `as_written_mut`, the `as_*_mut` accessors, `renumber`, `Dict::remove`). `Object::new`, `null`, `reference`, `at`, `value`, `as_bool`, `as_integer`, `as_number`, `as_reference` and `is_null`, `Data::at`, `Dict::new`, `WithStreams::new`, `Parser::at`, `window`, `in_stream`, `ciphertext`, `limits`, `rules` and `offset`, and `StreamHead::dict`, `keyword` and `start` are `const fn`, beside `ObjectId::new`, `Span::new`, `Place::new` and `Version::new`, so a value with nothing on the heap, placed or not, a dictionary with no entries and a parser and its settings can be made at compile time. Every function and trait method that gives a `Result` says what fails it and when, under `# Errors`: the parser's `value`, `header`, `body` and `stream`; the writer's nine and `Encrypt::string` and `stream`; `Resolve::try_resolve`; `Streams::decoded`; and `text::encode_as`. `Parser`'s doc shows one made and dropped, which the compiler refuses under `deny(unused_must_use)`.
- **What a call's values take in memory, said.** `Limits::values`' doc says what they take at worst on a 64-bit target: 288 bytes each, besides their names' and strings' bytes, as an `Object` is at most 72 bytes, a dictionary's `Entry`, a key and its value, at most 128, and an array of one value has room for four; so under the default 2^25, 9 GiB, from 64 MiB of arrays of one value nested in each other. A test holds the sizes to the doc on 64-bit targets.
- **Benchmarks.** `cargo bench -p pdf-objects` times the parser, the writer, dictionaries, the walks of references and text strings on inputs the benchmarks make as they run, the same on every run: indirect objects as a file's body holds them (a page, its content stream, the page tree, fonts and annotations), each read through its header and body and a stream's data by its `/Length`; arrays of numbers, of strings with escapes and of names with escapes; and dictionaries of 1,000 keys, each about 1 MB, by ISO 32000-2's rules and by PDFium's, with a sink that wants no faults and, by PDFium's, one that wants them; objects with 0x80 and 0xFF as white-space, by PDFium's rules; those objects, reals and strings written; a dictionary of 16 entries and one of 1,000, each with keys written twice, looked up, walked, its duplicates found and its keys counted; a page tree's references walked and renumbered; and text strings decoded and encoded in PDFDocEncoding, UTF-16BE and UTF-8. Throughput is in bytes, or in entries and references where nothing is read or written. So a change is judged before and after it. The harness is criterion, a dev-dependency never built for a user of the crate, and `benches/` is outside the package.
